Should a film be judged solely on its merits, or is the context surrounding its production important to consider as well? In this case, I believe Werner Herzog's achievement in crafting *Aguirre, the Wrath of God* warrants the latter. 

The titular Aguirre is based on a historical [Spanish conquistador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lope_de_Aguirre), who actually called himself the *Wrath of God*. Needless to say, Aguirre personifies the megalomania and greed emblematic of that era, during the age of discovery. While masterfully portrayed by Klaus Kinski, I'd argue that the main character of the film is not truly Aguirre, but mother nature instead. 

Throughout the film's runtime, sparse dialogue sequences are almost relegated to b-roll status as they are bookended by extended, captivating footage of the environment—the claustrophobia of the Amazonian jungle, footsoldiers hacking through dense vines to push forward, river rapids that threaten to capsize their overloaded, precarious rafts. 

At some point while watching the film, I noted to myself that it felt like I was watching a documentary about the Amazon rainforest instead. And indeed, that wouldn't be too far off considering Herzog's penchant for documentaries. He's made over 30 documentaries compared to 20+ feature films. 

In examining the film's technical aspects, it's difficult to imagine what it must have took to manage an extensive cast and crew throughout the depths of the Amazon—at the age of 30 when the film premiered, no less. The film's opening shot sets the bar. The image of a trail of footsoldiers, clad in armour, looking wildly out of place as they descend a treacherous Peruvian ridge. In the shot, the soldiers (and slaves, I should add) are trudging hesitantly.

This is a doomed journey from the start. Exacerbated by one man's desire to find the lost city of gold at any cost.
