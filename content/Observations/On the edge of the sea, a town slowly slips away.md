Earlier in April, I found myself in a small city along the eastern coast of Japan at the behest of my parents who wanted to sample the 'freshest catch' while visiting family in Tokyo. What I did not expect was to mourn for a town I was never a part of, a sense of bittersweet despair as I witnessed the fading of a community with its golden years behind it. 

Having visited Japan twice, I sought out experiences away from the large cities in favour of roads less travelled. A quick cursory search led me to discover Choshi, Japan's [top fishing port](https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/premium/supply-trade/japan-s-top-fishing-port-feeling-effects-of-rising-sea-surface-temperatures) in terms of sheer volume, which was merely a 2-hour drive away from Tokyo and close to Narita Airport where we would eventually depart from. 

While reading up on Choshi and planning a rough itinerary, I learnt of the town's struggles as it faced population decline, as was the case across most of rural Japan where residents eschew rural areas in favour of megacities like Tokyo and Osaka in search of better opportunities. 

Choshi is part of the Chiba prefecture, more commonly known by international arrivals via Narita Airport as a gateway to Tokyo, and for Tokyo Disneyland and DisneySea. For those unfamiliar, I'd also like to introduce you to what I think is the most adorable mascot in all of Japan, Chiba-kun. 

![[p1alr9ker05hpa86g7ao461ba25.jpg|359]]
> This goofy red dog, which was inspired by Chiba's outline, was created in 2011 as part of regional tourism efforts. 

Locals like to reference Chiba-kun's anatomy when describing where they're visiting. In this instance, I visited Choshi, which would be the tip of his ear. Narita Airport would be somewhere in the region of his brain, which I think is a perfect analogy in and of itself. Anyway, I digress.

Besides being a historic fishing port, Choshi is famed for its soy sauce production, with legacy brands like Yamasa and Higeta tracing their lineage back to as early as the Edo period in the 1600s.

Choshi is also known for its dramatic coastline as it overlooks the Pacific Ocean and it is one of the places you can catch the earliest sunrise across the whole of Japan, at Cape Inubo. Personally, I have always favoured the coast for how it demands your attention and pulls you into the present. There is something inherently meditative about sitting on rocks formed eons ago, listening to the relentless crashing of waves and feeling the sting of the ocean air on your face.

## Visiting Choshi

![[R0008367.webp]]

> Our unsuccessful attempt to catch the sunrise at Cape Inubo due to overcast weather. 

Despite having heard of the town's declining population, what I observed seemed more bleak than I could have imagined. (A word of caution, this was my experience on a Monday which may just have been a slow day, but other sources online seem to corroborate this fact, which I will draw from later.)

As we drove into town at noon, we barely saw anyone else on the streets on the main avenue. This was a stark contrast to being in Tokyo, where claustrophobia shadows you everywhere you travel. We went to a few of Choshi's main attractions, including Inubosaki Lighthouse, Enpuku-ji, and Byobugaura Cliffs, as part of our 1-night stay at a local Airbnb. And across all these places, there were never more than a handful of visitors at once.

The self-service cafeteria where we had lunch was neatly divided into big tables that could seat eight people each, and yet, at every single table, there sat only one—occasionally two—local diners who were just making a pitstop before going about their day. 

Despite initially feeling like I was finally able to breathe and reclaim some personal space after a week in cramped Tokyo, a sense of uneasiness slowly started stalking me. Granted, I visited on a weekday, but surely this can't be it during a peak travel season where millions flock to Japan catch the cherry blossoms?

Visitor arrivals in Japan hit an all-time high of 42.7 million last year, and yet there is little here to suggest prosperity. Unfortunately, tourists concentrated in hotspots like Tokyo and Kyoto it seems, to the detriment of regional areas such as this one.

> About 73% of overnight stays are packed into just five prefectures (Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, Hokkaido, Fukuoka). Source: [World Economic Forum](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/05/how-japan-is-redesigning-tourism-to-benefit-local-communities/)

Chiba, where I was visiting for two nights, has the second-lowest average length of stay in the country, at just 0.3 nights, inched out only by Nara at 0.2 nights. Despite being the gateway to international visitors via Narita Airport, most visitors see it only as a landing point to their next destination, likely Tokyo. 

Thus, despite record-breaking tourism tickets, very little bounty flows to rural cities like Choshi. And the plight of its very own rail infrastructure, the Choshi Electric Railway, will likely influence the fate of the town.

## The loss-making railway

Opened in 1923, the privately-owned Choshi Electric Railway Line is the city's only passenger rail. At its peak, the 6.4-km railway served over 2.5 million passengers annually, or about 7,000 passengers per day. These days, daily ridership are in the triple digits, with some stations even seeing as few as [11 passengers daily](https://www.city.choshi.chiba.jp/content/000068092.pdf). 

In order to stay afloat amid declining ridership, the railway has resorted to ingenious measures such as selling [naming rights](https://www.choshi-dentetsu.jp/railway/1254/) and senbei crackers, the latter which now accounts for up to 70 percent of revenue today.

Even the railway operator has started calling themselves the *Gakeppuchi* (cliff edge) Line, a double entendre referring to both the seaside cliffs around the town and the precariousness of the railway's fate. 

Naturally, I had to visit one of the stations to see for myself what the century-old railway looks like, and we drove to Tokawa Station. Built in 1921, it serves as the terminus station of the Choshi Electric Railway. As we pulled into the adjacent parking lot, it felt like we stepped into another dimension, with the tiny Showa-style building lending it an unmistakable sense of nostalgia.

Despite there being no one else but us inside the station, this place somehow felt full of life. The interior had such a charming, homely feel to it, adorned with cushioned benches, toy plushies, a small browsing library, gashapon machines, and local posters. What struck me was how much heart this place had, because you could clearly tell that whoever had a hand in decorating the interior wanted the best for their own little community.

So much so that as an outsider looking in, I could feel the love put into this space, a transit point meant for temporary rest while waiting to continue your journey. And it hurt to know that all this has lasted a century but may not last much longer. 

In a town as small as this, the train runs once per hour, and we arrived just 15 minutes before its scheduled arrival, so we naturally had to see it. I'm by no means a trainspotter, but the rolling stock that eventually pulled into the station looked significantly older than most trains that I'd been on at this point. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%8Dshi_Electric_Railway_Line#Rolling_stock), the trains are likely built in the 1960s for use in Tokyo, and were recently purchased and refurbished by the Choshi Electric Railway.

The train interiors were full of whimsy, instead of the utilitarian design that we've come to expect from public infrastructure. Sunflower motifs on the cushions in one car, a decal of a cat pawing a school of fish in another. It was not until then that I realised we could have had joyful, imaginative trains all along instead of the steel sardine cans we're used to by now.

Despite all this flair, there were only two other local tourists onboard the train before it departed on its return leg. And the only other people on the train were the conductors. 

How can any railway operator continue to justify running the trains for just a handful of passengers per station? It doesn't take an economist to tell that there simply aren't enough commuters to sustain this local train. 

The railway has had its fair share of struggle over the years, dealing with bankruptcy and embezzlement crises, as well as declining tourism ever since the Tohoku Earthquake in 2011, the same event that led to the Fukushima nuclear disaster. 

But if the railway were ever to go defunct, it may be the last straw, the final push over the metaphorical cliff edge, for Choshi. According to latest data, only about one-sixth (17%) of rail passengers are local residents, hence the railway's revenue is heavily dependent on tourism. So what now, when Choshi, like many other rural areas, are unable to reap the rewards of the tourism boom? 

## Trains are more than a means of transport

In the wider context of depopulation, tourism matters more than ever to such small communities. As it stands, should residents continue to leave rural areas, a compound effect starts to emerge. Businesses will continue to decline, local cultures and specialities are lost, revenues decrease, resulting in lower available funds for public infrastructure or development, making it even less attractive to stay, or visit.

And since tourists rely on the railway for sightseeing, then without it there would be even fewer reasons to come, leading to fewer opportunities for spending. Though residents may not fully utilise the railway, there are still some who are dependent on it as a lifeline. If the trains were ever to go, the effects would be disproportionately felt most by vulnerable populations—think elderly, disabled, students, those who aren't able to afford a car.  

This situation isn't unique to Choshi either, as across much of Japan, Japan Railways Group contemplates [winding down unprofitable lines](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2026/01/03/japan/jr-unprofitable-lines/). About 1,366 kilometres of track, or 5 percent of Japan's total network, has already been [discontinued](https://japantoday.com/category/national/rail-closures-total-1-366-km-in-japan-over-30-yrs-amid-depopulation) in the past three decades, with rural regions taking the brunt of it. 

Objectively, when viewed as a financial asset, it's easy to draw a conclusion from declining ridership and revenue to say that this train is no longer profitable because residents see no value in using it anymore, ergo it has fulfilled its need and has been replaced by alternative forms of transport. However, that viewpoint ignores the role of trains in the wider civic fabric. 

In my opinion, trains are in fact a public good, and should be viewed as necessary social infrastructure. Why? Because they democratise access to transportation.

Take libraries for example. Realistically, libraries are never profitable or seen as such, but we continue to fund them precisely because we acknowledge the value that they provide. Libraries democratise access to knowledge, by lowering the barriers to information through its free service. This service exists not because it maximises profit, but because it prevents exclusion. 

In the same vein, trains provide the following benefits:
1. It provides much needed mobility to student and elderly populations, connecting them to essential services. 
2. They play a part in connecting rural nodes with larger networks and metropolitan areas. Rural train lines are capillaries that feed into arterial rail services like the Shinkansen.
3. They promote a sustainable form of transportation, they consume significantly less energy and emit far fewer greenhouse gases than cars or planes. Especially relevant today given that we've just witnessed how quickly oil and gas can be taken away from us.
4. They foster a sense of community by providing a pseudo third space that physically connects individual journeys. As opposed to cars where people are isolated in metal shells.

Okay, that's enough about trains. I think you get my point. 

## What next?

The situation isn't all doom and gloom for Choshi and its railway. They've been fighting for their survival since the 2010s, and they will continue to.

The point I'm trying to make is that as tourists ourselves, we have an opportunity to make an impact by choosing to spend on experiences that matter. 

Rural revitalisation is more important than ever, not just in Japan but everywhere, lest we lose touch with disappearing means of production, lifestyles and cultures. 

As someone who has traveled solo a decent amount, I've had to opportunity to reflect on how to be a conscious traveller. Most of my memorable experiences have been at small businesses away from the beaten path, at places where locals are eager to get to welcome you as they struggle with competing against popular destinations. 

Social media and the algorithms have contributed greatly to the overtourism that has been plaguing popular destinations worldwide. We've lost the ability to discover things for ourselves, and that is precisely where the magic happens for me.

Instead of mindlessly following someone else's recommendation just because it is a must-visit or a hyped tourist spot, why not take some time to do your own digging to see what opportunities you might find? 

Personally, while planning this trip, I consulted a lot of tourism resources directly from Japanese government websites. These resources typically work with unique local businesses to create itineraries and showcase local crafts and delicacies, and they are very robust.

You don't have to necessarily visit Choshi like I did. There are numerous other beautiful rural towns out there in Japan, away from overcrowded Tokyo and Kyoto. Once you give these places a chance, I think you'd be pleasantly surprised.
